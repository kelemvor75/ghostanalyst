New-issue note & why we rate HOLD: SpaceX is a generational company — the dominant launch provider on Earth and the owner of Starlink, a genuine cash engine. But it has traded publicly for only four sessions, has no analyst track record, ~5% public float, and a Musk-controlled dual-class structure. After IPO'ing at $135 on June 12, the stock has rocketed to ~$192 (intraday $225, a ~$2.5–3T cap) — above every published analyst target — on a company posting a $4.9B annual loss. This is a world-class business at a price set by a float-driven scramble, not fundamentals. We initiate at HOLD, with the heavy caveats a four-day-old mega-IPO demands.
1Investment Thesis
  • A category-defining sum-of-the-parts: launch dominance + a Starlink cash engine + Starship optionality. SpaceX is three businesses in one. Launch (Falcon 9) commands ~90% of global commercial launch share (~$4.2B FY25 revenue), an effective monopoly anchored by NASA/NRO/USSF. Starlink is the world's largest satellite-broadband network — 10.3M+ subscribers across 164 countries on 9,600+ satellites, >$700M/month and growing — the high-margin (60–70% EBITDA) cash engine that anchors the valuation. Starship is the deep-space optionality (and the R&D money pit). No other company on Earth has this combination.
  • Real revenue, real growth — and real losses. FY25 revenue was ~$18.7B (up from $14.0B in FY24), and the business actually turned a GAAP profit in FY24 (+$0.8B). But FY25 swung to a $4.9B net loss on ~$3B of Starship R&D, and Q1 2026 alone reportedly lost ~$4.3B as investment accelerated. Analysts model Starlink revenue reaching $9–12B in 2026; the bull case is that the Starlink annuity scales into massive profitability while Starship opens entirely new markets (point-to-point, lunar/Mars, space-based data centers).
  • But the price has detached from fundamentals — hence HOLD. At ~$192 (a ~$2.5T cap), SPCX trades at roughly 130x+ trailing sales on a loss-making company, above the entire published analyst target range ($63–$227, consensus ~$164). The move is structurally driven: a ~5% float, forced index buying (Nasdaq-100 Fast Entry, MSCI), and insiders who legally cannot yet sell. A former Nasdaq chief publicly warned it is "not trading on fundamentals." We love the business and respect the franchise; at this price, after a four-day moonshot and ahead of a staggered lockup, the risk/reward is not compelling. HOLD.

    4Price Action & The Business Mix

    Four sessions of history: SPCX debuted at $135, closed its first day +19% at $161, spiked to an intraday $225.64 (briefly past a $3T cap on an overnight Robinhood print to $229), and trades near $192. The analyst consensus (~$164–178, dotted) sits below the price — the hallmark of a float-driven debut rather than a fundamentals-driven one. This is price action, not yet a trend.

    The sum-of-the-parts (estimated from reported figures): Starlink is the cash engine (~$11–12B run-rate at 60–70% EBITDA margins), Launch is the profitable monopoly (~$4.2B), and Starship/other is the optionality that consumes $3B+ of R&D and drives the consolidated loss. The valuation debate is entirely about how much to pay today for Starlink's scaling annuity plus Starship's uncertain, enormous TAM.

    5The IPO & Why The Stock Has Moved

    The largest IPO in history. SpaceX priced at a fixed $135/share on June 11 and debuted June 12 on Nasdaq, raising ~$75B at a ~$1.77T valuation — an all-primary deal with an unusually high ~30% retail allocation target but only ~5% of shares in the public float. In four sessions the stock has run ~42% above the offer (and was briefly up ~67%), pushing the market cap to ~$2.5–3T — at moments ahead of Amazon and Microsoft.

    The move is structural, not (yet) fundamental. Three mechanics explain the spike: (1) a ~5% float — tiny supply against enormous demand; (2) forced index buying — SpaceX qualifies for Nasdaq-100 "Fast Entry" (~15 trading days post-listing) and MSCI inclusion, compelling price-insensitive passive funds to buy; and (3) locked-up insiders who legally cannot sell yet. The same forces reverse over time: a staggered lockup releases insider supply from ~July (post-first-earnings) through December, mechanically adding float just as the index-buying tailwind fades. The stock is, for now, a supply/demand instrument with a calendar attached.

    The skeptics have a microphone too. A former Nasdaq chief publicly cautioned that SPCX is "not trading on fundamentals." Morningstar's independent fair-value estimate ahead of the IPO was ~$780B — less than half the IPO valuation and well under a third of today's ~$2.5T cap. First public earnings are expected around September 2, 2026 — the first real fundamental test.

    Competitive position — arguably the widest moat in the investable market. In launch, SpaceX has no peer at scale: Falcon 9's reusability gives it a cost-and-cadence advantage that Blue Origin (recent New Glenn failures), Rocket Lab (Neutron in development), and the legacy primes cannot match. Starlink's first-mover scale (9,600+ satellites) and vertical integration with its own launch capacity create a self-reinforcing flywheel rivals (Amazon's Kuiper, OneWeb) are years behind. The risks are not competitive so much as execution and valuation: Starship must work to justify the optionality, Starlink ARPU and margins must hold as it scales, and — uniquely — the company is entirely dependent on Elon Musk, who holds ~85% voting control and whose attention spans multiple companies.

    7Valuation — A Sum-of-the-Parts Bet on the Future

    SPCX cannot be valued on earnings (loss-making) or on any normal sales multiple (~130x+ trailing is meaningless). Like other Musk-led, narrative-driven names, it is best framed as a sum-of-the-parts where the price implies enormous future value for Starlink and Starship. At ~$2.5T, the market is paying roughly 3x the ~$780B independent fair-value estimate — i.e., capitalizing years of flawless execution today.

    8Management, Control & Capital Structure

    This is, to an unusual degree, a bet on one person. Founder, CEO & CTO Elon Musk controls SpaceX through a dual-class structure — Class B shares carry 10 votes each, giving him ~85% of the voting power while public Class A holders get economic exposure and "virtually no governance power." Musk is simultaneously running Tesla, xAI, X, Neuralink, and The Boring Company; his attention and key-person risk are real and concentrated. The board recently added Roelof Botha as an independent director, but make no mistake: SPCX holders are minority economic partners in a Musk-controlled enterprise.

    What would move us to Buy: (1) a pullback toward fundamental value (the $100–160 zone) that restores upside; (2) first earnings confirming Starlink's $9–12B trajectory at 60–70% margins; (3) a successful Starship orbital flight de-risking the optionality; (4) the float expanding and the stock holding through lockup releases (proving genuine demand, not just scarcity); (5) clear progress toward consolidated profitability.

    14Rating & Conclusion

    We initiate SpaceX at HOLD with a base-case 12-month target of $164 (the analyst consensus, below the current price). To be unambiguous about the company: SpaceX is one of the most remarkable enterprises ever built — an effective launch monopoly, the owner of Starlink (a genuine, scaling, high-margin cash engine), and the developer of Starship, which if it works opens markets that don't yet exist. As a business, it may have the widest moat in the investable market. Long-term, the bull case is serious and we take it seriously.

    But our rating is on the stock at today's price, and there the case for patience is overwhelming. SPCX has traded for four sessions, IPO'd at $135, and rocketed to ~$192 (~$2.5T) — above every published analyst target, at ~130x+ trailing sales, on a company that lost $4.9B last year, with ~5% float and ~85% Musk voting control. The move is the product of scarcity and forced index flows, not fundamentals — a dynamic that mechanically reverses as the staggered lockup releases insider supply through H2 2026. The risk/reward is asymmetric the wrong way: the base case sits below the price, and the bear case (toward the ~$780B independent fair value) is a severe re-rating. We would be enthusiastic buyers of this franchise — at a price that reflects it, after real price discovery. Today, at ~$192 on five-percent float ahead of the lockup cliff, the disciplined call is HOLD.

    Bottom line: HOLD, $164 base-case target (below spot). A generational company — launch monopoly + Starlink cash engine + Starship optionality — whose stock, four days into the largest IPO in history, has run above every analyst target on ~5% float and forced index buying, while the business still loses billions and Musk holds ~85% of the votes. Love the company; respect the price. Wait for the lockup-driven price discovery (H2 2026) or a pullback toward fundamental value before turning constructive.
