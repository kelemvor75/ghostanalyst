1Investment Thesis
  • The market is arguing about the numerator. Bulls call 8.4x forward earnings absurd. Bears answer that cyclical peaks always print low multiples right before the E collapses. Both camps are debating the same variable. Neither is pricing the one thing that actually changed this year: Sandisk has contracted away roughly half its bit output at enforceable floor prices, which does not protect peak earnings — it rebuilds trough earnings.
  • $93.9 billion of floor-priced minimums, with $16.5 billion of collateral behind it. Ten New Business Model agreements across eight datacenter and edge customers, weighted-average duration above four years, backed by $16.5B of collective financial guarantees — including $1.24B of contract liabilities, $1.5B of refundable security deposits and $5B of third-party collateral. Coverage steps from roughly 50% of bits in FY2027 to about two-thirds in FY2028.
  • Trough EPS moved from negative to plausibly positive — that is the re-rating case. Sandisk reported a GAAP loss in each of the three years before this one: diluted EPS of −$14.78 in FY2023, −$4.63 in FY2024 and −$11.32 in FY2025, against $73.76 in FY2026. A contracted floor book of roughly $21.8B per year means the next downcycle starts from a structurally different base. Cyclicals with positive, collateralized trough earnings deserve a higher through-cycle multiple than NAND has ever been granted.
  • It is not cheaper than Micron — and that is the point. Micron trades near 6.5x forward earnings against Sandisk's 8.4x. The relative case here is not headline cheapness; it is downside asymmetry. Sandisk is the only NAND-levered name whose floor is written into contracts rather than hoped for.
  • The bear case is real and we are not burying it. Q4 gross margin of 84.6% is not a durable number for a commodity manufacturer, and management told us two-thirds of the sequential revenue gain was price, not volume. Roughly 47% of FY2026 revenue shipped to China and Hong Kong. Insiders sold in a 70:1 ratio to purchases in the September quarter. The stock has risen 76% off its July low and sits 68% above its 200-day average.

    3What Just Happened — Price Action and the Index Bid

    Sandisk closed Friday, September 18 at $1,791.82, up 11.0% on the session and 9.7% on the week. The proximate cause was S&P Dow Jones Indices announcing that Sandisk will replace Colgate-Palmolive in the S&P 100, effective before the open on Monday, September 21. Index-tracking funds must buy, and in a name with 146 million shares outstanding and 133% trailing three-month realized volatility, forced demand moves the tape hard.

    That is a liquidity event, not a fundamental one, and it is important to separate the two. The fundamental picture underneath is that the stock set a closing high of $2,335.00 on June 25 (intraday peak $2,354.39), collapsed 56.5% to a closing low of $1,015.89 by July 29 on AI capital-expenditure doubts and memory supply-glut fears, and has since rallied 76.4% off that low. Today's price is still 23.3% below the June peak and down 18.0% over three months, even after a +132% six-month return.

    The July drawdown deserves scrutiny because it is the market's actual bear case expressed in price. Investors began questioning whether hyperscaler capital spending would translate into enterprise SSD procurement at the rate embedded in estimates, worried aloud about YMTC's rising layer counts and enterprise penetration, and marked down the whole storage complex. Sandisk fell further than Micron, Western Digital or Seagate because its revenue is the least diversified — it is NAND and enterprise SSD, full stop, which makes it the purest expression of NAND average selling prices available in the US market.

    Our read: the index add is a real near-term bid but a poor entry. An investor who buys Friday's close is paying up for flow that has already been announced. The fundamental case below is what justifies owning the name through the S&P 100 rebalance and out the other side.

    5Revenue Architecture and Segments

    Sandisk reports three end markets, and the FY2026 shape of them is the whole story. Datacenter revenue reached $5.15B, up 437% year over year, and accelerated to $2.98B in the fourth quarter alone — a 103% sequential jump. Edge, the largest bucket at $12.16B, grew 195%. Consumer, at $2.94B, grew 29% for the year but fell 32% sequentially in Q4 to $556M.

    That consumer decline is not a demand failure; it is allocation. When contracted datacenter and edge customers will pay several times the price per bit that a retail memory card commands, a capacity-constrained manufacturer starves the consumer channel. Management has been explicit that supply is the binding constraint. The read-through is that consumer revenue is now a residual — it tells you how tight the market is, not how the business is doing.

    Quarterly revenue as reported; Q1 FY2027 shown at the $10.55B guidance midpoint.

    Geographically, the concentration is the uncomfortable fact of this business. Hong Kong billings of roughly $5.1B and mainland China of roughly $4.5B together represent approximately 47% of FY2026 revenue. Hong Kong billing addresses in the memory industry frequently represent distribution into greater China and onward, so the true China exposure is difficult to pin precisely — but any honest framing places roughly half of Sandisk's revenue in a jurisdiction subject to export-control policy that has already reshaped the semiconductor trade twice this decade. We treat this as the single largest tail risk in the name, above pricing.

    Sequential revenue composition matters as much as the total. Management attributed approximately one-third of the Q4 sequential increase to higher volumes and two-thirds to higher pricing. That ratio is the most important disclosure in the release. It means roughly two-thirds of the incremental revenue — and, because pricing falls almost entirely to gross profit in an already-built fab, considerably more than two-thirds of the incremental profit — is a function of spot NAND prices rather than anything Sandisk controls.

    6The Contract Book — What NBM Actually Does

    This is the section that decides the stock, so it is worth being precise about the mechanics rather than the marketing. The New Business Model agreements are not fixed-price take-or-pay contracts. They combine fixed and variable pricing components, with contractual floors and ceilings on the variable portion. CFO Luis Visoso framed the aggregate as a minimum of $93.9B in expected revenue assuming floor pricing. Actual revenue is higher if the market clears above the floor, and capped in the other direction by the ceilings.

    Now the arithmetic the market has not done. Implied floor revenue of roughly $21.8B per year compares against FY2027 consensus revenue of $48.91B and against a current annualized run-rate of about $42B based on the Q1 guidance midpoint. In plain terms: the contracts underwrite roughly 45% of what consensus expects Sandisk to earn next year. They do not protect the 84.6% gross margin, and anyone telling you otherwise has not read the disclosure.

    What they do protect is the floor, and the value of that is best understood by looking at what this business actually did in the years immediately before this one.

    Three consecutive years of GAAP losses — cumulatively more than $30 per share — and then $73.76. A 7.1% gross margin in FY2023 is what a NAND manufacturer looks like when spot pricing sits below cash cost and there is nothing contractual standing underneath it. That is the history the market is extrapolating when it assigns a single-digit multiple to FY2027 earnings, and the market is right to remember it.

    The change is that a contracted book delivering roughly $21.8B annually, at floor prices negotiated during the tightest NAND market in the industry's history, is a categorically different starting point for the next downturn. Floors struck in 2026 sit far above 2023 clearing prices. The next trough should not look like FY2023, and that — not the current 84.6% margin — is what an investor is actually buying.

    The $16.5B of financial guarantees is the detail that separates this from a press release. Contracts are only worth the counterparty behind them, and a hyperscaler facing collapsed spot prices has every incentive to renegotiate. Sandisk holds $1.24B of contract liabilities and $1.5B of refundable deposits in cash, with a further $5B of third-party collateral. That is real security against precisely the scenario in which the contracts matter most.

    The differentiated conclusion. The correct question for a cyclical is never 'what multiple on peak earnings' — it is 'what is trough EPS, and how confident am I in it?' Sandisk's trough EPS was negative in each of the three years through FY2025. Today a collateralized contract book underwrites roughly $21.8B of annual revenue at floor pricing. That does not make Sandisk a utility, and we are not going to pretend it does. It does mean the distribution of FY2028 outcomes has had its left tail cut off in a way that no prior NAND cycle offered, and that a business with a positive, contracted trough deserves a higher through-cycle multiple than the 2016–2023 industry ever received.

    Two structural items extend the argument without carrying the thesis. First, the August 27 announcement that Kioxia and Sandisk will invest more than $31B — roughly ¥5 trillion — through 2032 across the Yokkaichi and Kitakami plants, explicitly contingent on Japanese government support. This is simultaneously the bull case (co-funded, subsidy-supported, multi-year bit growth from a 25-year partnership that has already deployed over $50B in Japan) and the bear case (capacity is capacity, and every bit added is a bit that must clear). Second, High Bandwidth Flash: Sandisk has taped out its first HBF die, targeting 2027 samples and 2028 mass production, with a consortium including Google, Meta, SK hynix and Tenstorrent. The claimed specification — up to 512GB in 8-high and 16-high stacks at roughly 0.4 to 3.0 TB/s — would deliver HBM-class read bandwidth at eight to sixteen times the capacity. We assign HBF zero value in our base case. It is 2028 revenue at the earliest, Mizuho is publicly skeptical it displaces HBM outright, and a thesis that requires unshipped silicon is not a thesis.

    7Margin Structure and the Inflection Tell

    Sandisk's GAAP gross margin ran 29.8%, 50.9%, 78.4% and 84.6% across the four quarters of FY2026. Operating income moved from $192M to $7.04B over the same span. This is operating leverage in a fab-based business doing exactly what the textbook says: once capacity is built and depreciating, incremental price falls to the bottom line at close to 100%.

    GAAP gross margin and diluted EPS as reported by quarter; Q1 FY2027 shown at guidance midpoints (non-GAAP EPS).

    Here is the tell. Q1 FY2027 gross margin is guided to 83.0% to 84.9%, a midpoint of 83.95% against a Q4 actual of 84.6%. For the first time in five quarters, management is not guiding margin expansion. Revenue is still guided up 17.6% sequentially at the midpoint, so this is not a demand statement — it is a pricing statement. Either mix is shifting toward contracted bits carrying structured rather than spot pricing, or spot is flattening, or both. All three readings point the same direction: the rate of change in pricing has peaked even as the level remains extraordinary.

    That is not bearish on its own. A business printing 84% gross margins and guiding 84% gross margins is not deteriorating. But momentum investors are paid on second derivatives, and the second derivative of Sandisk's margin turned in this guide. It is the single most useful forward-looking number in the August 5 release, and it did not appear in a single piece of sell-side commentary we reviewed.

    The long-term model management laid out on August 13 is the counterweight. For FY2028 through FY2030 the company targets mid-to-high-teens revenue growth in line with bit growth, non-GAAP gross margin of approximately 80%, operating margin of approximately 75% with opex near 5% of revenue, and adjusted free cash flow margin around 50%, alongside a commitment to return 100% of excess cash after reinvestment. We regard the 75% operating margin target as aspirational rather than underwritable — no NAND manufacturer has ever sustained anything close — but we note the company is guiding to margin compression from today's levels, which is at least intellectually consistent.

    The comparison that matters is Micron. At roughly 6.5x forward earnings against Sandisk's 8.4x, Micron is the cheaper memory stock on headline multiples, with a bigger balance sheet, DRAM and HBM diversification, and a trailing revenue base of $90.3B against Sandisk's $20.25B. An investor who simply wants memory beta should probably own Micron, and we would not argue otherwise.

    Sandisk earns its place for a different reason. It is the only name in the complex whose downside is written into enforceable contracts with $16.5B of collateral standing behind them. Micron's floor is whatever the DRAM and NAND spot markets decide it is. That distinction is worth a premium to Micron's multiple, not a discount — and the market is currently applying a modest premium of about 1.9 turns, which strikes us as roughly fair to slightly stingy given the contract disclosure.

    The structural competitive risk is YMTC. Chinese NAND capacity is advancing on layer count and yield and is pushing into enterprise. YMTC does not need to be cost-competitive to damage the cycle; it needs only to be willing to ship at prices that clear. Combined with the $31B of Japanese capacity Sandisk and Kioxia themselves are adding through 2032, plus whatever Samsung and SK hynix bring, the supply response to 84% gross margins is already in motion. Margins this high are, in the end, an instruction to the industry to build.

    9Valuation and Sensitivity

    We value Sandisk on a multiple of FY2027 consensus earnings, with the cycle view stated explicitly rather than buried in a terminal-growth assumption. Consensus for the fiscal year ending July 2027 sits at $212.62 in non-GAAP EPS on $48.91B of revenue. Our target of $2,125 applies a 10.0x multiple to that figure. Every forward multiple in this report is struck on non-GAAP consensus; the trailing 24.3x is on GAAP. The two bases are close for Sandisk — FY2026 GAAP diluted EPS of $73.76 against non-GAAP of $70.88 — but they are not identical, and GAAP was the higher of the two.

    Why 10.0x and not more. A 10x multiple on peak-cycle earnings is roughly where memory has historically been allowed to trade when investors believe the peak is real but not durable. It happens to be the same multiple the market applies to Sandisk's own annualized Q1 guidance — $45.00 midpoint times four is $180, which at today's price is 9.95x. In other words, the market is currently paying for the Q1 run-rate and nothing beyond it, while consensus expects FY2027 to come in 18% above that run-rate. Our target captures the consensus ramp; it does not capitalize the peak.

    The grid's message is that the multiple matters less than the earnings. Moving from 8.5x to 11.5x at consensus EPS swings the implied share price from $1,807 to $2,445 — meaningful, but a 35% range. Moving FY2027 EPS from $170 to $260 at a constant 10.0x swings it from $1,700 to $2,600, a 53% range. This is an earnings-estimate stock, not a multiple-expansion stock, and the estimate is a function of NAND pricing that nobody — including management — can forecast with confidence.

    On cash flow: applying the company's own approximately 50% adjusted free-cash-flow margin target to FY2027 consensus revenue implies roughly $24.5B, a free-cash-flow yield near 9.0% on enterprise value. With zero long-term debt, $15.5B of remaining buyback authorization after the board added $14B, and a stated policy of returning 100% of excess cash, the capital-return math is genuinely supportive. We note the company repurchased $4.52B of stock during FY2026 — a portion of it at prices well below today's.

    One caution on the consensus curve itself. FY2028 consensus EPS of $261.77 is followed by FY2029 at $261.59 — flat — and then FY2030 at $456.50 on $98.84B of revenue. That FY2030 figure rests on thin coverage and implies a revenue base nearly five times FY2026's. We would not underwrite anything past FY2028, and readers should treat the outer years as a sparse-estimate artifact rather than a forecast.

    Insider selling is the most uncomfortable fact in this report and we are not going to soften it. The disposed-to-acquired ratio for the September quarter is 70:1 — 52 sales against zero purchases. In September alone, Form 4 filings disclosed roughly $51.7M and $1.6M of sales on September 17 and $4.1M on September 9. Some portion of this is almost certainly scheduled 10b5-1 activity and the mechanical monetization of equity that has appreciated nineteen-fold in twelve months; after a move of that magnitude, concentration risk in an executive's personal balance sheet is a legitimate reason to sell. But zero purchases across an entire quarter, by anyone, is a signal in its own right. If management believed the stock were materially undervalued at these levels, we would expect to see at least one open-market buy. We see none.

    The February 2026 secondary is a separate matter and a cleaner one. 5,821,135 shares priced at $545 on February 18, arising from a debt-for-equity exchange involving Western Digital. Sandisk sold no shares and received no proceeds. It was not dilutive, and anyone treating it as a capital raise has misread the filing. We flag it because our pre-publication screen surfaced it as a potential dilution event and it is worth stating plainly that it was not one.

    We want to be direct about the asymmetry inside this table. Four of the eight risks carry high impact, and the two we rate highest on probability — spot pricing reversal and industry supply response — are the same risk viewed from two angles. Sandisk is a price-taker in a commodity whose price has risen to a level that guarantees a supply response. The contract book changes how far the fall goes; it does not change whether there is one.

    The risk we would most want readers to sit with is the China and Hong Kong concentration. Roughly half of revenue billed into a jurisdiction where semiconductor trade policy has been rewritten twice this decade is not a risk that scenario analysis handles well, because the distribution is not continuous. There is no elegant mitigant. It is a reason to size the position smaller than the valuation alone would suggest.

    15What Would Change Our Mind

    We would move to HOLD on any of the following. A fiscal Q2 2027 gross-margin guide below 78%, which would convert the flattening we flagged into a decline and put FY2027 consensus of $212.62 out of reach. Evidence that NBM customers are renegotiating volumes or invoking exit provisions, which would break the floor that underpins the entire differentiated case. Contracted bit coverage failing to reach roughly 50% in FY2027 on schedule. Or a confirmed export-control action materially restricting shipments into China and Hong Kong — that alone would take roughly half the revenue base into question and no multiple would hold through it.

    We would move to SELL if spot NAND contract prices decline more than 30% from current levels while the company simultaneously confirms that non-contracted bits are clearing at or below floor pricing. That combination would mean the ceiling provisions inside the NBM agreements are capping the recovery while the floors are being tested on the way down — the worst configuration of the contract structure, and one that would justify re-basing to trough earnings immediately.

    Conversely, we would upgrade toward STRONG BUY on a Q2 guide holding gross margin above 84% with revenue above $11.5B, any open-market insider purchase after four quarters of pure selling, or NBM coverage reaching two-thirds of bits ahead of the FY2028 schedule. The first would mean pricing has not peaked; the second would mean the people with the best information disagree with their own selling; the third would mean the annuity is arriving faster than the market has modeled.

    16Conclusion

    Rating: BUY. Price Target: $2,125, representing 18.6% upside from the September 18 close of $1,791.82. The target applies a 10.0x multiple to FY2027 consensus EPS of $212.62, the fiscal year ending July 2027.

    The case for owning Sandisk is not that it is cheap. At 8.4x forward earnings it is more expensive than Micron, and a single-digit multiple on a memory company at a cycle peak is the market's standard way of saying it does not believe the earnings. The case is that Sandisk has done something no NAND manufacturer has done before: it has sold roughly half of its future output forward, at contractual floors negotiated during the tightest market in the industry's history, and collected $16.5B of guarantees to make those floors enforceable. That converts trough earnings from the −$11.32 per share the company actually reported in FY2025 into something plausibly in the $80s. A cyclical with a positive, collateralized trough is a different security than a cyclical without one, and it should not trade at the same through-cycle multiple.

    What we are explicitly not underwriting: the 84.6% gross margin, the 75% long-term operating margin target, High Bandwidth Flash revenue, or the FY2030 consensus figures. Each of those is upside to a base case that requires only that FY2027 consensus be delivered.

    What we are explicitly warning about: this is a 133%-realized-volatility stock that has risen 76% in seven weeks and gained 11% on Friday on an index-inclusion announcement. The entry is poor. Roughly 47% of revenue bills into China and Hong Kong. Insiders sold 52 times and bought zero times last quarter. The bear case is a 41% drawdown to $1,050. Anyone building a position here should do it in tranches and should own the stock for the contract book, not for the momentum — because the momentum is the part that can reverse on a Monday.
